# Workflow Automation Consulting for Accounting & Bookkeeping Firms
## Complete Curriculum

---

## 0. How to use this document

This is your map, not your textbook. The teaching happens in conversation, one lesson at a time. Come back here to see where you are and what's next.

Two rules that matter more than the content:

**Rule 1 — You are studying a business, not a technology.** By the end of this you should be able to walk into a 12-person accounting firm and, within 45 minutes of conversation, tell the owner something true about their own business that they had not articulated themselves. That is the bar. Tool knowledge does not get you there.

**Rule 2 — Every concept gets attached to money.** When you learn what a "bank reconciliation" is, you have not finished learning it until you know how long it takes, who does it, what it costs the firm, what happens when it's late, and what breaks when it's wrong.

## 1. Changes I made to your proposed framework

You gave me 12 stages. I'm giving you 14. Here is what changed and why, because you should understand the reasoning rather than just accept it.

**Added: Security, Compliance & Trust (now Stage 7).** You had security as one bullet inside "Solution Design." In this industry it is not a design detail — it is a *sales gate*. Accounting firms hold Social Security numbers, bank credentials, payroll data, and tax records. In the US, firms are legally required under the FTC Safeguards Rule and IRS Publication 4557 to maintain a Written Information Security Plan, and their insurers and clients ask about it. In the UK and EU, GDPR applies. A firm will end a promising conversation the moment you sound casual about where client data goes. You need this knowledge *before* you start selling, not during delivery.

**Promoted: Software Ecosystem to its own stage (now Stage 4).** You had it as a topic. It deserves a stage because it is the fastest credibility win available to you. If you can say "you're on Xero with Dext and Karbon, so I'm guessing your document chasing lives half in Karbon and half in someone's inbox" — you have skipped three months of trust-building.

**Merged: Positioning into Selling (Stage 13).** How you package and price *your own* services is inseparable from how you sell them.

**Moved earlier: ROI thinking.** You had ROI as Stage 8. ROI arithmetic is simple; the hard part is doing it habitually. You'll start doing it in Stage 5 and it becomes reflex by Stage 10.

**One thing I'd push back on in your prompt:** you wrote that your goal is to "eventually sell." Don't wait. From roughly Stage 5 onward you should be having real conversations with real firm owners — not selling, just asking. Twenty conversations with actual practitioners will teach you more than Stages 6 through 9 combined, and this curriculum is far less valuable if you study it in isolation for six months first.

## 3. Stage-by-stage detail

### Stage 1 — Understand the Industry

**What you should be able to do at the end:** explain to a non-accountant what a bookkeeping firm does, how it differs from a CPA firm, and why that difference determines everything about how each one operates.

Lessons:
1. **The Work Ladder** — the four rungs of firm work (transaction processing → close & compliance → assurance → advisory), and why price, margin, and automatability change as you climb
2. Firm types and what each is legally allowed to do — bookkeeper, CPA/CA, Enrolled Agent, tax agent, audit firm
3. Firm sizes and how behaviour changes: solo, micro (2–5), small (6–25), mid (25–100), and why your first customers live in a specific band
4. Core accounting vocabulary you must never fumble — general ledger, chart of accounts, journal entry, trial balance, accruals vs cash basis, the three financial statements
5. The compliance calendar and seasonality — busy season, extension season, quarterly filings, and what this does to a firm's willingness to buy or change anything
6. The staffing crisis — why firms structurally cannot hire their way out of capacity problems, and why this is the single biggest tailwind for your business
7. Outsourcing and offshoring — how firms already use offshore teams, and how that shapes how they'll perceive you

**Exercise:** Read three real firm websites (one bookkeeping-only, one small CPA firm, one "CAS-first" firm). For each, write down what services they sell, what they charge if published, who they seem to serve, and one thing that tells you how they're positioned. Bring me your notes.

---

### Stage 2 — Understand the Economics

**What you should be able to do at the end:** look at a firm's headcount, client count, and service mix and estimate its revenue, its constraint, and where it's losing money — then check yourself by asking three questions.

Lessons:
1. Pricing models — hourly, fixed-fee, value pricing, subscription — and the operational consequences of each
2. **Effective hourly rate** — the single most important number in this industry, and why fixed-fee firms often don't know theirs
3. Utilization and realization — what "chargeable hours" means, typical targets, and why they're both gamed
4. Write-offs, write-downs, WIP and lock-up — where revenue quietly disappears
5. Client profitability and the long tail — why most firms have 20% of clients destroying margin and can't name them
6. Capacity as the real constraint — the shift from "we need more clients" to "we need more hours"
7. Revenue per employee, partner profit, and the benchmarks owners compare themselves against
8. Acquisition, retention and churn — what a client is worth over its life, why churn is low but recovery is slow
9. Seasonality and cash flow — why the calendar dictates when firms buy

**Exercise:** I give you a firm's headcount, client list and fee schedule. You calculate revenue, effective hourly rate per service line, and identify the loss-making service. Then I show you what you missed.

---

### Stage 3 — Understand Operations

**What you should be able to do at the end:** draw any of the core firm workflows from memory, including the exception paths, and name the bottleneck in each.

Lessons cover each workflow end to end using the format `Client → Inputs → Process → People → Software → Output → Review → Delivery`:

1. Client acquisition: lead → discovery → proposal → engagement letter → onboarding
2. Client onboarding — the most-broken process in most firms
3. Monthly bookkeeping and the transaction lifecycle
4. Bank reconciliation and the uncategorized-transaction problem
5. Month-end close and close checklists
6. Accounts payable and approval chains
7. Accounts receivable and collections
8. Payroll
9. Tax preparation and the PBC ("prepared by client") document cycle
10. The review and approval bottleneck — why manager/partner review is the constraint in almost every firm
11. Financial reporting and management reporting
12. Client communication, chasing and escalation
13. Billing, WIP management and invoicing
14. Offboarding and data handover

**Exercise:** I describe a workflow in messy narrative form, the way an owner would actually say it. You produce a clean process map with steps, owners, systems, handoffs, decision points, and exceptions.

### Stage 4 — Understand the Software Ecosystem

**What you should be able to do at the end:** hear a firm's stack and immediately know what's likely broken between the tools.

Categories, each studied as *"what problem does this solve and what does it fail to solve"*:

- **General ledger:** QuickBooks Online, Xero, Sage, NetSuite, MYOB, Zoho Books
- **Practice management / workflow:** Karbon, Canopy, Financial Cents, Jetpack Workflow, TaxDome, Pixie, Xero Practice Manager
- **Document capture & OCR:** Dext, Hubdoc, AutoEntry
- **Close management & QA:** Keeper, Uncat, Xenett
- **Document request & client portal:** Content Snare, Liscio, SmartVault, ShareFile
- **AP:** Bill, Ramp, Melio, ApprovalMax
- **AR & collections:** Chaser, Satago, native reminders
- **Payroll:** Gusto, ADP, Paychex, Rippling, BrightPay
- **Expense & cards:** Expensify, Ramp, Brex, Pleo
- **Tax:** Drake, Lacerte, ProConnect, UltraTax, CCH Axcess, TaxCalc
- **Proposals, engagement & billing:** Ignition, GoProposal, Anchor
- **Reporting & advisory:** Fathom, Syft, Spotlight, LivePlan
- **Automation platforms:** Zapier, Make, n8n, Power Automate, Workato
- **AI layer:** LLM-based extraction, categorization, drafting, anomaly detection

Critical sub-topic: **API reality**. Which of these have usable APIs, which are read-only, which are locked down, which have rate limits that will ruin your architecture, and which force you into brittle workarounds. This determines what you can honestly promise.

**Exercise:** Given three firm stacks, identify for each the most likely integration gap and the manual work that gap creates.

### Stage 5 — Understand Problems

**What you should be able to do at the end:** hear a complaint and name the underlying cause, plus two plausible alternatives.

Lessons:
1. The universal pain list and why it's universal — chasing clients, review backlog, rework, status opacity, deadline anxiety, data re-entry
2. Symptom vs cause — "we need reminder emails" is a symptom
3. Rework and its hidden cost — the transaction touched four times
4. Coordination overhead — the tax on every handoff
5. Status opacity — why owners can't answer "where is that client's work right now?"
6. Exception load — the 15% of clients consuming 60% of the effort
7. Tribal knowledge and key-person risk
8. The arithmetic of waste: **frequency × duration × people × loaded cost**
9. Problems automation cannot fix — bad pricing, no process, wrong clients, understaffing at the review layer

**Exercise:** I give you five real complaints in an owner's own words. You produce a root-cause hypothesis for each and the question you'd ask to test it.

---

### Stage 6 — Understand Automation Patterns

**What you should be able to do at the end:** map any workflow onto `Trigger → Input → Rules → Action → Exception → Human review → Output → Audit trail`, and pick the right level of intervention.

Lessons:
1. The three tiers — simple automation, workflow orchestration, AI-assisted judgment — and when each is appropriate
2. Trigger design: event, schedule, state change, and the underrated "absence of an event"
3. Data mapping and identity — the client-matching problem across five systems that all name the same client differently
4. Rules and conditions; when a rules engine beats a model
5. **Exception handling** — the difference between a demo and a system
6. Human-in-the-loop design — where approval genuinely belongs and where it's just anxiety
7. Idempotency, retries and failure modes — what happens when the API is down mid-run
8. Audit trails and reversibility — non-negotiable in this industry
9. Where AI genuinely earns its place: unstructured document extraction, classification with confidence scoring, anomaly detection, draft generation
10. **Where AI is the wrong answer** — anything where a silent wrong answer is worse than no answer
11. Cost of ownership — who maintains this in 18 months

**Exercise:** Take one workflow from Stage 3. Design three interventions at three tiers. Argue for the cheapest one.

### Stage 7 — Security, Compliance & Trust

**What you should be able to do at the end:** answer a firm's security questionnaire without flinching, and explain your data handling in three sentences an owner will accept.

Lessons:
1. What data accounting firms actually hold and its sensitivity tiers
2. Regulatory baseline by market — FTC Safeguards Rule and IRS Pub 4557 / WISP requirements (US), GDPR (UK/EU), Privacy Act and TFN rules (AU)
3. Professional confidentiality obligations and why the firm's licence, not just its reputation, is exposed
4. Data residency, sub-processors, and the questions you will be asked about where AI inference happens
5. Access management — least privilege, credential handling, and never asking for a shared login
6. Audit trail requirements and record retention
7. What SOC 2 is, when you'll be asked for it, and what to say before you have it
8. Independence rules — why audit clients constrain what you may build
9. Your own liability: engagement terms, professional indemnity insurance, scope limitation
10. Writing a one-page security summary that closes deals

**Exercise:** Write your own data handling one-pager. I'll play the sceptical managing partner.

### Stage 8 — Business Diagnosis

**What you should be able to do at the end:** run a structured process audit and produce findings a firm would pay for on their own.

Framework: **People · Process · Technology · Data · Bottlenecks · Repetition · Exceptions · Risk · Economics · Opportunity**

Lessons:
1. Scoping an audit — what you look at and, more importantly, what you refuse to look at
2. Interviewing at three levels: owner, manager, doer — and why their accounts will contradict each other
3. Observation over description — what to ask to see rather than hear about
4. Process mapping notation you can draw live in a meeting
5. Quantifying without a time-tracking system
6. Building the opportunity register: impact, effort, risk, dependency
7. Prioritisation — sequencing, quick wins, and the trap of starting with the biggest problem
8. Presenting findings without insulting anyone, especially the person who built the current process

**Exercise (recurring):** A full fictional firm audit, increasing in complexity each time. You produce the diagnosis. I mark it.

### Stage 9 — Solution Design

**What you should be able to do at the end:** produce a design document a firm could hand to any competent engineer.

Each design covers: **Current state · Problem · Future state · What's automated · Human-in-the-loop · Technology · Data flow · Exceptions · Security · Rollback · Success metrics · ROI**

Lessons:
1. Designing for the 80% and routing the rest to humans
2. Choosing build vs configure vs buy — and when the honest answer is "buy the off-the-shelf tool, you don't need me"
3. Phasing: what ships in week two versus month four
4. Change management and adoption — the reason most of these projects fail
5. Measurement design — instrumenting the process so you can prove value later
6. Documentation and handover
7. Designing for the firm's busy season

---

### Stage 10 — ROI & the Business Case

**What you should be able to do at the end:** build a defensible business case in fifteen minutes on a call.

Lessons:
1. Loaded cost per hour — the number most people get wrong
2. Hard savings vs soft savings vs capacity creation, and which to lead with
3. **The honesty problem:** freed hours are not automatically billable hours. Learn to ask "what would you actually do with the time?" before claiming a number
4. Error and rework cost, including the cost of a missed filing deadline
5. Revenue-side value: faster turnaround, higher client capacity per staff member, retention
6. Payback period, and why anything over 9 months is a hard sell to a small firm
7. Building the model in a spreadsheet the client can edit — this builds more trust than a polished PDF
8. Defending your assumptions under challenge

---

### Stage 11 — Client Discovery

**What you should be able to do at the end:** run a 45-minute call where the owner does 70% of the talking and finishes it feeling understood.

Lessons:
1. Structuring the call: context → business → process → pain → impact → priority → next step
2. Question banks for each phase, and the follow-up questions that matter more than the openers
3. Listening for the tell — vague answers, defensiveness, "it depends," and long pauses
4. Quantifying live without interrogating
5. Diagnosing the buying process: who decides, who blocks, what's the budget reality
6. Recognising when to disqualify, and why doing it early makes you more credible
7. The trap of premature solutioning
8. Ending with a specific, small, committed next step

**Exercise (recurring):** Live roleplay. I play owners with different personalities — the sceptic, the enthusiast who wants everything, the one who's been burned before, the one who won't give you numbers.

Run at least one small real engagement, paid or unpaid, before Stage 13. Everything you believe about this business will be revised here.

Topics: scoping tightly, building in the firm's environment, testing with real messy data, training users, handling the week-three "it broke" moment, monitoring, and support expectations.

### Stage 13 — Positioning, Packaging & Selling

Lessons:
1. Choosing your beachhead — firm size, market, service line, stack. Narrow beats broad, badly
2. Productizing: audit offer, implementation projects, ongoing retainer
3. Pricing your own services — why hourly billing will trap you exactly like it traps them
4. Proposal structure: business problem → impact → solution → ROI → scope → price
5. Common objections and what's actually behind them — "too expensive," "we tried this before," "my team won't use it," "what if you disappear"

## 5. Skills to develop

**Business:** reading a P&L, unit economics, capacity modelling, benchmark literacy

**Domain:** accounting vocabulary, the compliance calendar, service delivery mechanics, regulatory basics

**Analytical:** process mapping, root-cause analysis, quantification under uncertainty, prioritisation

**Technical:** API integration, the major automation platforms, data mapping and reconciliation logic, LLM extraction with confidence handling, error handling and observability, security engineering

**Consulting:** structured interviewing, active listening, written synthesis, presenting to sceptics, saying no

**Commercial:** proposal writing, pricing, objection handling, scope control

The one to prioritise if you can only pick one: **structured interviewing.** It is the bottleneck for engineers entering consulting, and it's the least likely to develop on its own.

## 6. Common mistakes beginners make

1. **Leading with technology.** "I build AI automations" tells an owner nothing about their business.
2. **Automating a broken process.** You'll make bad work happen faster and get blamed for the result.
3. **Believing the stated requirement.** "We need reminder emails" is almost never the actual problem.
4. **Ignoring the review bottleneck.** Speeding up preparation when review is the constraint produces zero improvement and a bigger queue. This is Theory of Constraints and it will bite you repeatedly.
5. **Claiming savings that don't materialise.** Freed hours that nobody redeploys are worth nothing, and the client will notice in month four.
6. **Underestimating exceptions.** Your demo handles the clean case; the firm's reality is 30% messy.
7. **Being casual about data.** One sloppy remark about where client data goes ends the conversation.
8. **Selling during busy season.** Nobody in a US tax firm is buying anything in March.
9. **Assuming the owner knows their own numbers.** Many can't tell you their least profitable client. That gap is itself a product.
10. **Building something only you can maintain.** If it breaks while you're unreachable, you've created a liability.
11. **Skipping change management.** Staff who weren't consulted will route around your system.
12. **Going too broad.** "Automation for accounting firms" is not positioning. "Month-end close orchestration for QuickBooks-based CAS firms with 5–20 staff" is.
13. **Recommending AI where determinism is required.** A silently miscategorised transaction is worse than an unprocessed one.
14. **Not disqualifying.** A firm with no process, no budget and no internal champion will consume six months and pay you nothing.
